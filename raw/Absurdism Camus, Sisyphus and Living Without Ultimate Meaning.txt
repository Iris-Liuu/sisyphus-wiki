Absurdism is the philosophical view, developed above all by Albert Camus, that the human demand for meaning and the silence of the universe are permanently at odds, and that the honest response is neither to deny the clash nor to escape it, but to keep living with it in full awareness. The "absurd" is not a property of the world or of people taken alone. It is the relation between the two, and Camus's answer to it, set out in The Myth of Sisyphus (1942), is lucid revolt rather than despair or faith.

What is absurdism?
Absurdism holds that human beings look for ultimate meaning, order and justification, while the world offers no answer that we can recognise. Camus calls the gap between the two "the absurd." Neither side is at fault. A world that simply goes on being what it is would not be absurd, and a creature that never asked "what is all this for?" would not feel the absurd either. It arises only when the question meets the silence.

Three features separate absurdism from its neighbours:

It is a relation, not a verdict. The absurd lives in the confrontation between a questioning mind and a mute world. Remove either term and it disappears.
It refuses both comforts. Camus rejects the religious or ideological hope that supplies a meaning from outside, and he equally rejects the conclusion that nothing is worth doing.
It is a way of living, not a theory of everything. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy presents Camus's alternative to suicide and hope as living without appeal, with lucidity and revolt, with Sisyphus as its emblem (see its entry on Albert Camus).
Absurdism is usually grouped with the mood of existentialism, but Camus denied that he was an existentialist, a point taken up below. For the full side-by-side, see Existentialism vs Nihilism vs Absurdism.

Where the idea comes from
Several earlier writers circle the experience Camus names, though none is an "absurdist" in his sense.


Kierkegaard uses "the absurd" in a different way. In Fear and Trembling (1843), the pseudonymous author Johannes de silentio presents Abraham, ready to sacrifice Isaac, as a figure whose faith looks absurd from outside. For Kierkegaard the absurd marks what reason cannot justify and faith nevertheless embraces. For Camus it marks a dead end that should not be embraced at all. The shared word hides an opposite use, which is why Camus treats Kierkegaard as a case of the move he criticises.

Dostoevsky matters through his characters. Camus discusses Kirillov in Demons in The Myth of Sisyphus as a man who pushes the logic of a godless world to suicide, which is exactly the conclusion Camus wants to test and avoid.

Kafka is the writer whose fiction stages the absurd most vividly: trials without charges, castles without access. Camus devoted an appendix of The Myth of Sisyphus to him, "Hope and the Absurd in the Work of Franz Kafka," arguing that Kafka's work hints at a hope that the absurd, strictly kept, should not allow.


The sense of a silent or indifferent universe is also familiar from philosophical pessimism, and from Schopenhauer in particular, but pessimism ends in a verdict about the value of existence, where absurdism keeps the question open.

The Myth of Sisyphus (1942)
Camus wrote The Myth of Sisyphus in occupied France and it appeared in 1942, the same year as his novel The Stranger. The essay opens with a famous claim: there is only one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide. Judging whether life is or is not worth living, he says, answers the fundamental question of philosophy. Everything else, such as whether the world has three dimensions, comes afterwards.

The question is a test of the absurd. If the world has no meaning that we can find, is there any reason to go on? Camus reviews the possible answers and sorts them into three. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarises them as physical suicide, philosophical suicide, and accepting the absurd while continuing to live (see its entry on Camus).


1. Physical suicide
Suicide ends the problem by ending one of its two terms. Camus argues that it does not resolve the absurd but evades it. Since the absurd is a confrontation, removing the person who confronts the world does not answer the confrontation; it simply stops it. To accept the absurd, in his view, is to stay at the point of tension.

2. "Philosophical suicide": the leap
The second answer is subtler, and Camus finds it in respectable philosophy. A thinker feels the clash between reason and the silent world, then jumps to something beyond reason that restores meaning: God, a transcendent order, a hidden harmony. Camus calls this "philosophical suicide," because it kills the lucid mind that noticed the absurd in the first place. The Stanford Encyclopedia names the thinkers he places in this camp: Shestov, Kierkegaard, Jaspers and Heidegger, along with Husserl's phenomenology, on the ground that each starts from the absurd and then turns to something transcendent.


Whether Camus reads these thinkers fairly is disputed. Kierkegaard in particular did not regard faith as an escape from the tension, but as a way of enduring it. The use of the leap as a target tells us more about Camus's own position than about theirs. For the thinker who does take the leap, see fideism.

3. Revolt, freedom and passion
The remaining option is to keep both terms in view and live. Camus gives this stance three consequences, usually remembered as revolt, freedom and passion:

Revolt: a constant refusal to be reconciled, neither pretending that meaning exists nor surrendering to its absence.
Freedom: if no higher purpose fixes our lives in advance, we are free of the ready-made roles and goals that usually organise them, though not free in the metaphysical sense debated in the free will and determinism literature.
Passion: since quality of experience matters more than a final accounting, the aim becomes to live as much as possible, to multiply experience rather than to ration it.
Sisyphus as the absurd hero

The essay ends with the Greek myth. The gods condemned Sisyphus to roll a rock up a mountain, only for it to fall back each time. Camus makes him the absurd hero. He is conscious of his fate, and in the moment when he walks back down to the rock, he is superior to it, because he knows exactly what he is doing. The essay concludes that "one must imagine Sisyphus happy." The line is not a claim that the punishment is pleasant. It says that the struggle itself can fill a life, once the demand that it be redeemed by some outcome is dropped.

From individual revolt to collective revolt: The Rebel (1951)
The Myth of Sisyphus describes a solitary lucid individual. Camus's later book The Rebel (L'Homme révolté, 1951) carries the same attitude into politics and history. It asks whether revolt can justify murder, and examines how revolutions founded on a promise of justice have turned into new forms of domination, including Marxism-Leninism.


The turn is that revolt is no longer only a private posture. When someone says "no" to an intolerable condition, Camus argues, they implicitly affirm a value that other people share. The Stanford Encyclopedia gives the formula as "I revolt, therefore we are," in place of the individual certainty of Descartes. Revolt therefore sets limits: it cannot legitimately destroy the very human solidarity from which it springs. This is the basis of Camus's wariness toward revolutionary violence.

Was Camus an existentialist?
Camus is routinely filed beside Sartre as an existentialist, and he repeatedly denied it. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that he rejected the label throughout his career. The two men were friends and allies after the war, and Sartre accepted the label for himself while describing Camus's thought as a "philosophy of the absurd."

The substantive difference is this. Sartre's existentialism holds that we create meaning through free choice and commitment, so that existence precedes essence and we are "condemned to be free." Camus starts from the same silent universe but declines to say that we manufacture a meaning to fill it. For him the honest stance is to live without a meaning, not to invent one. The longer comparison, including how absurdism differs from nihilism, is in Existentialism vs Nihilism vs Absurdism. In short, the nihilist concludes that nothing matters, while the absurdist keeps the demand alive.

The "theatre of the absurd"
The word is also used in literary criticism, with a related but distinct meaning. The critic Martin Esslin coined "the theatre of the absurd" in a 1960 essay and developed it in a book of 1961. He grouped dramatists such as Samuel Beckett, Eugène Ionesco, Arthur Adamov and Jean Genet, and drew on Camus's account of a world stripped of illusions to explain their plays.

The two uses differ. Camus's absurd is a philosophical relation that a person lives through. The theatre of the absurd is a dramatic form in which plot, language and purpose break down, so that the audience feels the lack of meaning rather than hearing it argued.

Objections to absurdism
Nagel: the clash is inside us. In "The Absurd" (Journal of Philosophy, 1971), Thomas Nagel accepts that life is absurd but locates the source differently. For Nagel the absurd comes from a collision between the seriousness with which we take our lives and the permanent possibility of seeing everything we care about as arbitrary or open to doubt. That puts the conflict within us and not between us and the world. It also changes the right response. Nagel finds Camus's defiant stance romantic and self-pitying, and proposes irony instead of heroism or despair: if nothing matters, then that does not matter either.

Sartre and the political break. After The Rebel, Camus fell out with Sartre and the circle around the journal Les Temps Modernes. The quarrel was set off by criticism of the book's stance on revolution and communism; accounts differ on the exact chronology, so the date is left open here. The objection from Sartre's side was that Camus's revolt, with its limits on violence, let history off the hook: abstaining from revolutionary commitment in the name of moral purity could protect existing injustice. Camus replied that the end cannot excuse every means.

Is it a stable position? Critics also ask whether "revolt without hope" can be kept up. If the world is really silent, why should lucidity be a virtue, and why should life be preferred to death at all? Camus treats lucidity as a starting stance rather than proving it, and this is where defenders and critics divide.

Is the demand for meaning real? Some philosophers dissolve the problem from the other side, arguing that a lack of cosmic meaning is not a loss, since a life can be meaningful on a human scale without an ultimate guarantor. On that view the absurd is a mistake about what meaning requires. Others, from the traditions of theism, reply that the demand for meaning is itself evidence that the world is not silent.

Related views
Nihilism — the denial that anything has meaning or value; absurdism rejects the conclusion while sharing the starting premise.
Existentialism — meaning is created by free choice; Camus separated himself from it.
Fideism — the leap to faith that Camus calls philosophical suicide.
Pessimism — the case that existence is, on balance, bad.
Stoicism — acceptance of what we cannot change, but within a rational cosmic order that absurdism denies.
Atheism — the denial of God, which absurdism presupposes without being identical to.
For the author himself, see Albert Camus, and for a reading order, Where to Start with Camus. Nietzsche, with his reading of the "death of God," is the other great backdrop to the debate.

Frequently asked questions about absurdism
What is absurdism in simple terms?
It is the view that humans look for meaning in a universe that does not provide any we can find, and that this mismatch is the absurd. The proper response, Camus argues, is to live fully and consciously with it, without suicide, religious faith or denial.

Who founded absurdism?
No single person founded it, but Albert Camus gave it its classic form in The Myth of Sisyphus (1942). Earlier writers such as Kierkegaard, Dostoevsky and Kafka dealt with related experiences, and Thomas Nagel offered an influential variant in 1971.

What does "one must imagine Sisyphus happy" mean?
It means that Sisyphus, who knows his task is endless and futile, can still find fullness in the struggle itself. His happiness does not come from the rock reaching the top. It comes from lucid acceptance of his fate and from refusing to hope for an outcome that will never arrive.

Is absurdism the same as nihilism?
No. Nihilism concludes that nothing has meaning or value. Absurdism starts from the lack of ultimate meaning but keeps asking for it, and insists that life is still worth living. The full comparison sets out the differences.

Is absurdism a form of existentialism?
It is often grouped with it, but Camus denied being an existentialist. Existentialists such as Sartre argue that we create our own meaning; Camus says we must live without one and not pretend to supply it.

What are the three responses to the absurd?
Camus names physical suicide, "philosophical suicide" (a leap of faith or other escape to a transcendent meaning), and revolt. He rejects the first two as evasions and takes the third, joined with freedom and passion, as the honest course.

What is the difference between Camus's absurd and Kierkegaard's?
For Kierkegaard the absurd is what reason cannot grasp, and faith accepts it. For Camus it is the standing conflict between human longing and a silent world, which must be held open and not resolved by faith.

What did Nagel say about the absurd?
In 1971 he argued that absurdity comes from a collision within us, between taking our lives seriously and being able to doubt everything we take seriously. His conclusion was that irony, not Camus's defiance, is the fitting response.
